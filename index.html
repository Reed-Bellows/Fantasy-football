How the rank is built

Each player gets three overall ranks for the chosen scoring format. The board's rank is the average of the three, so a player all three sources like rises to the top.

The small label next to each source rank is that source's position rank (WR7, RB12 and so on).

Where the numbers come from

FantasyPros: Rest-of-season expert consensus rankings (ECR) for PPR, Half PPR and Standard.

ESPN: ESPN's 2026 season projection minus points already scored, scored three ways (1, 0.5 or 0 per catch). K and DST use ESPN's own scoring.

Sleeper: Sleeper's free weekly projections for Weeks 4 to 18, summed. Player ages and injury tags also come from Sleeper's player data.

Turning points into ranks

Raw projected points overrate quarterbacks in a one-QB league, so ESPN and Sleeper overall ranks use value over replacement for a 12-team league: each player's points minus the QB12, RB30, WR36, TE14, K12 or DST12 at his position.

Projections of 0 usually mean the source expects the player to miss the rest of the season or has no role for him.

How the weekly rank is built

Weekly rankings are positional, the way start/sit decisions are made. Pick a position to see QB1, RB1 and so on, or FLEX to compare running backs, receivers and tight ends on one list.

The board's rank is the average of the three sources' ranks at that position (or in the FLEX pool).

Where the numbers come from

FantasyPros: Weekly expert consensus by position, plus their FLEX rankings, for each scoring format.

ESPN: The average of ESPN's analysts' weekly position rankings (PPR and Standard; Half PPR blends the two). ESPN publishes no FLEX list, so its FLEX rank uses ESPN's weekly projected points.

Sleeper: Sleeper's weekly projected points.

Game status

Once a player's game has kicked off, the row is marked and the Actual column shows his ESPN-scored points as of when the data was pulled. Green beats ESPN's projection, red falls short.
